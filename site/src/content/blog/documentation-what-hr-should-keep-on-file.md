<p>Documentation lives in the shadows of benefits administration. It’s the work nobody celebrates, the file you’re convinced you’ll never need, and the thing you wish you had when a problem finally surfaces.</p>

<p>But when it comes to Medicare and coordination of benefits, documentation can be the quiet protector between you and a compliance problem that results in costly recovery actions, coverage disputes, or <a href="/blog/medicare-part-a-hsa-conflicts-what-employers-overlook">potential penalties</a>. It can also be the thing that helps an employee resolve an incorrect penalty or coverage issue with Medicare.</p>

<p>Good documentation doesn’t just help you comply with the rules. It helps prove you complied. And that difference matters.</p>

<p>Documentation is the paper trail that says you did what you were supposed to do. When questions come up years later, it’s a whole lot easier to pull a file than try to reconstruct what happened from memory.</p>

## Medicare Notices and Creditable Coverage Determinations

<p>One of the most important Medicare-related notices for employers is the <em>Medicare Part D Creditable Coverage Notice.</em></p>

<p>Employers and other plan sponsors offering prescription drug coverage generally need to notify Medicare-eligible individuals whether that coverage is considered “creditable,” meaning it is expected to pay, on average, at least as much as standard Medicare Part D coverage.</p>

<p>And this isn’t just a retiree issue.</p>

<p>Depending on the plan, these notices can apply to Medicare-eligible active employees, retirees, <a href="/blog/cobra-vs-medicare">COBRA participants</a>, disabled individuals, and eligible dependents covered under the prescription drug plan.</p>

<p>These aren’t optional communications. When the disclosure requirement applies, employers need to provide the notice within the required timeframe.</p>

<p>HR should keep records showing when notices were distributed and how they were delivered. Some companies maintain copies of mailed notices while others keep electronic distribution records or email records.</p>

<p>CMS doesn’t generally require an employee to sign an acknowledgment or provide a read receipt, but keeping reasonable proof of when and how required notices were distributed is a smart practice.</p>

<p>It is also important to distinguish between keeping a copy of the notice itself and keeping evidence that the notice was actually distributed. Both can be useful when questions arise later. </p>

<p>For example, if an employee asks several years later whether they were told that their <a href="/blog/employer-medicare-20-employee-rule-ohio">employer prescription coverage</a> was creditable, having the applicable notice and distribution record is much more useful than simply having a current version of the company's notice. </p>

## Employee Acknowledgments and Coverage Decisions

<p>Beyond providing required notices, it can also be wise to document the Medicare education and information employees receive.</p>

<p>This is where employee acknowledgment forms can become valuable.</p>

<p>Did the employee receive information about Medicare enrollment? Did they understand that Medicare Secondary Payer rules determine whether Medicare or the employer plan pays first? Did they understand the potential consequences of delaying Medicare enrollment? Did they understand their <a href="/blog/do-you-need-medicare-if-you-still-have-employer-coverage">options for remaining on employer coverage</a>?</p>

<p>These acknowledgments don’t have to be elaborate. A simple written acknowledgment that an <a href="/the-employee-medicare-guide">employee received Medicare-related information</a> can be useful documentation.</p>

<p>As a reminder, employees and <a href="/blog/medicare-for-employers-when-medicare-should-have-been-primary">employers can’t choose which plan is primary</a>.</p>

<p>Medicare Secondary Payer rules determine whether Medicare or the employer plan pays first based on factors such as current employment status, employer size, and the reason the individual is entitled to Medicare.</p>

<p>Employees may decide whether to enroll in Medicare or remain on employer coverage, but they don’t elect which payer is primary.</p>

<p>When an employee makes an important coverage decision, document it. What was the date? What information was provided? Who explained the options?</p>

<p>If questions surface later, having that conversation documented can be incredibly valuable.</p>

### A Practical Example

<p>Imagine an <a href="/blog/what-employers-need-to-know-when-employees-turn-65">employee approaches age 65 </a>and asks HR whether they should enroll in Medicare or remain on the company's health plan.</p>

<p>Rather than documenting only the employee's final election, HR can maintain a record showing that Medicare-related educational information was provided, what materials were shared, when the discussion occurred, and what coverage decision the employee ultimately made.</p>

<p>The goal isn't to create unnecessary paperwork. It's to create a reasonable record of the information and process surrounding an important benefits decision.</p>

## Creditable Coverage Disclosures and Compliance Records

<p>If your prescription drug plan covers Medicare-eligible individuals, keep copies of your creditable or non-creditable coverage determinations and disclosures.</p>

<p>Keep the notices you provide to employees and other eligible individuals. Keep records of when they were distributed. And keep records of your annual disclosure to CMS.</p>

<p>Plan sponsors generally must disclose their plan’s creditable coverage status to CMS annually, typically within 60 days after the beginning of the plan year, as well as following certain changes or termination of prescription drug coverage.</p>

<p>This documentation matters.</p>

<p>Imagine a former employee enrolls in Medicare Part D three years from now and needs to prove they had creditable prescription drug coverage while working for you. You don’t want HR trying to reconstruct the answer from memory.</p>

<p>You want the file.</p>

<p>Employers should never manufacture or backdate a creditable coverage determination that wasn’t made or supported at the time. But if a former employee requests documentation of previous coverage, HR should review the records from that period and provide accurate historical information when the plan’s creditable status can be substantiated.</p>

<p>That’s another reason keeping good records today matters years from now.</p>

### Coordination of Benefits Decisions and Why Documentation Matters

<p>When Medicare and an employer plan overlap, Medicare Secondary Payer rules determine who pays first.</p>

<p>That determination isn’t discretionary.</p>

<p>Depending on the circumstances, the employer plan may be primary and Medicare secondary, or <a href="https://www.exactmedicare.com/blog/how-to-choose-the-best-medicare-plan-for-your-lifestyle">Medicare may be primary</a> and the employer plan secondary.</p>

<p>HR should maintain documentation supporting how the employee’s coverage was handled, including relevant employment status, plan enrollment information, communications with the employee, and other information used to determine how the MSP rules applied.</p>

<p>This documentation protects the employee because it helps establish what coverage they had and when.</p>

<p>It protects the employer because it creates a record showing that coverage wasn’t handled randomly. There was a process, documentation, and a consistent application of the rules.</p>

<p>There is also a separate Medicare reporting obligation known as Section 111 mandatory reporting.</p>

<p>Under Section 111, applicable Group Health Plan Responsible Reporting Entities report certain coverage information to CMS so Medicare can correctly determine whether Medicare or another plan has primary payment responsibility.</p>

<p>Depending on how your health plan is structured, HR may not personally handle that reporting. It may be handled by your insurer, TPA, or another responsible entity.</p>

<p>But HR should understand who is responsible for it and make sure the organization has a process for providing accurate information when needed.</p>

<p>Because without documentation, you’re trying to explain years of coverage decisions from memory.</p>

<p>And “I think that’s what happened” isn’t much of a compliance strategy.</p>

## What Should HR Keep in the Medicare File?

<p>There isn't one universal file structure that works for every employer or plan. Documentation requirements can vary <a href="/blog/coordination-of-benefits-how-employer-plans-and-medicare-decide-who-pays-first">depending on the plan, the employer's responsibilities</a>, and the individuals involved.</p>

<p>However, a practical Medicare documentation process may include:</p>

<ul><li>Copies of applicable creditable or non-creditable coverage determinations</li><li>Copies of Medicare-related notices provided to employees or other eligible individuals</li><li>Records showing when and how required notices were distributed</li><li>Medicare education materials used with employees</li><li>Records of employee communications regarding Medicare</li><li>Employee acknowledgments, when used</li><li>Relevant coverage elections or declinations</li><li>Employment-status information relevant to coordination-of-benefits determinations</li><li>Documentation supporting who is responsible for applicable Section 111 reporting</li><li>Records of corrections, changes, or follow-up communications</li></ul>

<p>The important point is consistency.</p>

<p>A documentation process is much more useful when HR knows what belongs in the file, who is responsible for maintaining it, and how records can be retrieved later.</p>

## How Long Should HR Keep Medicare Documentation?

<p>Retention periods should not be treated as a one-size-fits-all number.</p>

<p>Different records may be subject to different legal, regulatory, contractual, or plan-specific retention requirements. Employers should therefore establish a retention policy that considers applicable federal requirements, state requirements, plan documents, and other recordkeeping obligations.</p>

<p>The practical lesson is simple: don't delete important Medicare documentation simply because the employee is no longer active.</p>

<p>Former employees may need historical coverage information years after leaving the company. Having an organized archive makes it much easier to respond accurately.</p>

<p>HR should also make sure electronic records are stored securely and can be retrieved when needed. Medicare-related documentation may contain sensitive employee information, so access should be limited to appropriate personnel.</p>

## Build a Documentation Process Before You Need It

<p>The best time to organize Medicare documentation isn't after an employee raises a coverage dispute.</p>

<p>Create the process before the problem occurs.</p>

<p>Assign responsibility for Medicare-related records. Establish consistent naming and storage conventions. Keep copies of required notices and distribution records together. Document significant <a href="https://www.exactmedicare.com/services/additional-coverage">employee communications and coverage</a> decisions. And periodically review the process to make sure records are being maintained consistently.</p>

<p>This can be especially important for employers with growing workforces or employees approaching Medicare eligibility every year. What works informally for a small HR department can become difficult to manage as the organization grows.</p>

<p>A documented process also helps reduce the burden on HR. Instead of relying on one person who remembers <a href="https://www.exactmedicare.com/blog/how-exact-medicare-helps-you-find-the-right-plan-step-by-step">how Medicare questions</a> were handled, the organization has a repeatable system.</p>

## Frequently Asked Questions

### What Medicare documentation should HR keep on file?

<p>HR should generally keep copies of applicable Medicare-related notices, creditable or non-creditable coverage determinations, distribution records, employee communications, coverage decisions, and documentation related to coordination of benefits. The exact records an employer needs to maintain can vary depending on the <a href="https://www.exactmedicare.com/services/medicare-advantage-plans">health plan and applicable requirements</a>.</p>

### Does an employer need an employee’s signature to prove they received a Medicare notice?

<p>Not necessarily. CMS does not generally require an employee signature or read receipt for every Medicare-related notice. However, employers should maintain reasonable records showing what notice was provided, when it was provided, and how it was distributed when documentation of the communication may be important later. </p>

### How long should employers keep Medicare-related records?

<p>There is no single retention period that applies to every Medicare-related record. Employers should consider applicable federal and state requirements, plan documents, contractual obligations, and other recordkeeping rules when establishing their retention policies. Important historical coverage records should remain accessible for as long as they may reasonably be needed.</p>

### Can an employee and employer decide which plan pays first?

<p>No. Medicare Secondary Payer rules determine whether Medicare or an employer-sponsored health plan is the primary payer based on factors such as employment status, employer size, and the reason the individual is entitled to Medicare. Employees and employers cannot simply choose which coverage is primary.</p>

### Why is Medicare documentation important after an employee leaves the company?

<p>Former employees may need historical coverage information to address Medicare enrollment, prescription drug coverage, penalties, or other coverage questions. Maintaining organized records allows an employer to provide accurate information instead of trying to reconstruct years-old decisions or communications from memory.</p>
